\subsection{Sample Collection and Preparation}

Samples will be collected from an architectural facade exposed to the campus
environment to capture real-world degradation. The glass samples will be cut to
uniform dimensions and cleaned via standard protocols (e.g., detergent wash, DI
water rinse, and nitrogen drying) to establish a baseline.

For pollutant studies, samples will be kept pristine or artificially aged under
controlled conditions (e.g., exposure to 80--90\% relative humidity with trace
levels of \ce{SO2} and \ce{NO2} for 1--7 days) to simulate realistic
atmospheric aging and control the thickness of the adsorbed water film and
chemical contaminant layer.

\subsection{Choice of Nonlinear Spectroscopic Technique}

We will utilize Vibrational Sum-Frequency Generation (VSFG) spectroscopy.
VSFG is an even-order nonlinear optical process that is inherently
surface-specific, as it is symmetry-forbidden in centrosymmetric bulk media but
allowed at interfaces where the inversion symmetry is
broken~\cite{jubb2012environmental}.

Unlike linear infrared reflection or Raman scattering, VSFG selectively probes
only the top few molecular layers, making it ideal for distinguishing the
molecular signal of surface water and adsorbed pollutants from the bulk glass
substrate~\cite{jubb2012environmental,shen2016fundamentals}.

\subsection{Optical Setup and Experimental Geometry}

The optical setup requires a dual-beam geometry where a tunable infrared (IR)
beam (\qtyrange{2.5}{10}{\um}) and a fixed visible beam (\qty{532}{\nm}, from a
Nd:YAG laser) are overlapped spatially and temporally at the glass surface,
typically at incidence angles of $\sim \qty{55}{\degree}$ and
$\sim \qty{60}{\degree}$ relative to the surface normal, respectively.

The standard experimental arrangement utilizes four common polarization
combinations: ssp, sps, pss, and ppp. The ssp combination is the most widely
utilized for high-intensity surface sensitivity~\cite{jubb2012environmental}.

The effective second-order susceptibility for each polarization is determined
by the Fresnel factors $L_\text{ii}$ and the incident/output angles
$\theta_\text i$. For the four standard polarization
combinations~\cite[p.~108]{jubb2012environmental}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fresnel}
  \begin{aligned}
    \chi_\text{eff, ssp}^{(2)} &
  = L_{yy}(\omega_\text{SFG})L_{yy}(\omega_\text{vis})L_{zz}(\omega_\text{IR})
    \sin\theta_\text{IR} \chi_{yyz}, \\
    \chi_\text{eff, sps}^{(2)} &
  = L_{yy}(\omega_\text{SFG})L_{zz}(\omega_\text{vis})L_{yy}(\omega_\text{IR})
    \sin\theta_\text{vis} \chi_{yzy}, \\
    \chi_\text{eff, pss}^{(2)} &
  = L_{zz}(\omega_\text{SFG})L_{yy}(\omega_\text{vis})L_{yy}(\omega_\text{IR})
    \sin\theta_\text{SFG} \chi_{zyy}, \\
    \chi_\text{eff, ppp}^{(2)} &
  =-L_{xx}(\omega_\text{SFG})L_{xx}(\omega_\text{vis})L_{zz}(\omega_\text{IR})
    \cos\theta_\text{SFG}\cos\theta_\text{vis}
    \sin\theta_\text{IR} \chi_{xxz} \\
    & \quad-L_{xx}(\omega_\text{SFG})
            L_{zz}(\omega_\text{vis})
            L_{xx}(\omega_\text{IR})
      \cos\theta_\text{SFG}\sin\theta_\text{vis}
      \cos\theta_\text{IR} \chi_{xzx} \\
    & \quad+L_{zz}(\omega_\text{SFG})
            L_{xx}(\omega_\text{vis})
            L_{xx}(\omega_\text{IR})
      \sin\theta_\text{SFG}\cos\theta_\text{vis}
      \cos\theta_\text{IR} \chi_{zxx} \\
    & \quad+L_{zz}(\omega_\text{SFG})
            L_{zz}(\omega_\text{vis})
            L_{zz}(\omega_\text{IR})
      \sin\theta_\text{SFG}\sin\theta_\text{vis}
      \sin\theta_\text{IR} \chi_{zzz}.
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The Fresnel relations in \zcref{eq:fresnel} are needed to correct for the
experimental geometry when extracting intrinsic molecular information.

\subsection{Molecular Origin of the Nonlinear Spectroscopic Signal}

The signal arises from the second-order nonlinear polarization induced at the
interface
\begin{equation}\label{eq:P2}
  \mathbf P^{(2)}(\omega_\text{SFG})
= \chi^{(2)} : \mathbf E(\omega_\text{vis})\mathbf E(\omega_\text{IR}).
\end{equation}
The effective second-order susceptibility contains both resonant and
non-resonant contributions
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chi_sum}
  \chi_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}
= \chi_{\text{NR}}^{(2)} + \sum_{\nu} \chi_{\nu}^{(2)}.
\end{equation}
As shown in~\zcref{eq:I_SFG}, the measured VSFG intensity is proportional to
the square modulus of this effective susceptibility~\cite{jubb2012environmental}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:I_SFG}
  I_\text{SFG} \propto |\chi_\text{eff}^{(2)}|^2 I_\text{vis} I_\text{IR}.
\end{equation}
The signal is dominated by the molecular hyperpolarizability $\beta$,
which reflects the transition moments of the vibrational modes (Raman and IR
active) at the surface, providing structural orientation
information~\cite{jubb2012environmental}.

\subsection{Signal Analysis and Molecular Information}

The resonant contribution of each vibrational mode is described by a
Lorentzian lineshape:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lorentzian}
  \chi_{\text{eff}, \nu}^{(2)} \propto
  \frac{A_\nu}{\omega_{\text{IR}} - \omega_\nu + i\Gamma_\nu}.
\end{equation}
where $A_\nu$ is the transition strength, $\omega_\nu$ the vibrational
frequency, and $\Gamma_\nu$ the linewidth.

In conventional VSFG, however, the measured intensity
$|\chi_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}|^2$ loses the sign (phase) of the vibrational
transition. To recover the absolute molecular orientation, phase‑sensitive SFG
(PS‑SFG) measures the imaginary part of the resonant susceptibility
directly~\cite[Eq.~9]{jubb2012environmental}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:imag_chi}
  \Im \chi_\nu^{(2)} = -\sum_\nu
  \frac{A_\nu \Gamma_\nu}{(\omega_{\text{IR}} - \omega_\nu)^2 + \Gamma_\nu^2}.
\end{equation}
This approach in~\zcref{eq:imag_chi} allows unambiguous determination of the
up/down orientation of surface species.

By analyzing the peak positions (e.g., dangling \ce{O-H} vs.
hydrogen-bonded \ce{O-H}) and their relative intensities, we can quantify the
degree of surface water ordering, identify specific pollutants, and assess the
degradation state of protective coatings on the architectural
glass~\cite{jubb2012environmental}.
